Built Like Someone Who Was Staying

When the current owner moved into this house, the first thing he did was rip out every inch of plumbing and start over. Not because it was leaking. Because he was going to live here for the rest of his life and he didn't trust what he hadn't put in himself. That decision, made eight and a half years ago, defines what kind of house this is.

Water in Cabo is a real concern for most homeowners. Trucks, rationing, inconsistency. This house is connected to municipal water — 24 hours a day, seven days a week — and it also has a 10,000-liter underground holding tank as a backup. That tank is fed by two independent well pumps, each with its own backup. If the municipal supply goes out, the house doesn't notice. If one pump fails, the second one takes over. If both fail, the backups run. Nobody has ever tested that system because it has never needed to be tested.

Then there is the septic situation. Most homes on this corridor run a single system. This house has three — one for the kitchen, one for the laundry, and one for the bathrooms. Each is sized and dedicated to its function. Behind all three is a master overflow backup, so if any individual system had a problem, the waste would route to the backup before anything surfaced. In fifteen-plus years, nothing has surfaced. The system was overbuilt because the owner was staying.

The windows and doors in this house came from Germany. Double-pane, insulated, custom-ordered and installed throughout. Standing inside this house with every window closed, you cannot hear the wind. In Cabo, that is not a small thing. The corridor gets wind. Everyone on this stretch knows it. You feel it when you drive through, you feel it when you walk outside. Inside this house, you don't feel it at all. The silence is one of the first things visitors notice.

Hurricane season in Baja California Sur is real, and most homes on this corridor handle it with improvisation — plywood, sandbags, whatever is available. This house has hurricane shutters made in Florida, measured to every opening on the upper level of the structure. The investment was $30,000. They are not decorative. They close, they lock, and when they are down, the house is sealed. The current owner had them manufactured because he was planning to be here for storms. The next owner inherits that preparation.

The drinking water in this house is filtered twice before it reaches any faucet. There is a two-stage system — a sediment and chlorine filter at the pump outside, then a second dedicated filter under the kitchen sink. Every tap in the house runs filtered water. This is not a Brita pitcher situation. It is a system the owner designed because he drank from those taps every day.

The pool is PebbleTech. This matters more than it sounds. Tile pools crack. The grout breaks down. The tiles pop off. In Cabo's climate — the heat, the UV, the salt air — tile pools require constant maintenance and eventual resurfacing. PebbleTech is a different material entirely: an aggregate surface that doesn't crack, doesn't require regrouting, and doesn't turn a maintenance task into a seasonal project. The pool looks the same as it did when it was installed. It will look the same in ten years.

The painting — every wall, inside and out — was done by the owner himself. Not to save money. Because he wanted it done correctly. The exterior has been repainted twice since he moved in, each time with proper surface preparation and weather-appropriate materials. The palms in the yard were planted by hand, one by one. The landscaping was chosen and placed deliberately, not inherited from a developer's template. Every part of this property reflects the same logic: a man building a home he intended to live in forever, making decisions accordingly.
